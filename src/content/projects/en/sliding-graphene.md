Sliding Graphene · Cornell Lab — Exploring the landmark condensed-matter breakthrough reported in *Nature Nanotechnology* (published October 14, 2026; DOI: 10.1038/s41565-026-02306-x) by researchers at Cornell University: voltage-driven interlayer sliding and nonvolatile magnetic switching in tetralayer graphene.

Changing a solid material's crystal structure conventionally requires breaking robust covalent chemical bonds and reconfiguring atomic planes—an energy-draining, slow process demanding immense pressures (>10 GPa) and extreme temperatures (>1000 °C). In graphene, however, while intra-sheet carbon atoms are welded together by strong in-plane covalent bonds, the stacked sheets are held only by gentle, whisper-thin van der Waals interactions.

In the laboratory of Kenji Yasuda (assistant professor in the School of Applied and Engineering Physics at Cornell University), lead author and doctoral student Daniel Brandon discovered that an electric voltage applied across **four-layer (tetralayer) graphene** causes adjacent atomic planes to slide sideways relative to one another.

This lateral displacement alters the stacking symmetry: the material transitions from its most common and stable **Bernal configuration (ABAB)** to the metastable, exotic **rhombohedral configuration (ABCA)**. While Bernal tetralayer graphene is completely nonmagnetic, the rhombohedral stacking aligns electronic flat bands near the Fermi surface, activating intense electron-electron interactions that ignite spontaneous **ferromagnetism**—alongside strong tendencies toward superconductivity and correlated quantum states.

Crucially, this electric switching is **nonvolatile**: once slid into the rhombohedral or Bernal arrangement, the atomic stack remains firmly locked in place after the driving voltage drops to zero. A reverse voltage pulse smoothly slides the layers back to Bernal nonmagnetism. This reversible, low-energy sliding offers a compelling paradigm for rewritable nonvolatile atomic memory and programmable quantum hardware.

Co-authors on the paper include Cornell doctoral student Yiwen Ai; Akshat Gandhi, M.Eng. '25; postdoctoral researcher Lujin Min; undergraduate Kyle Nadel '28; research intern Peter Golemis; along with collaborating physicists from Stanford University, Stony Brook University, and the National Institute for Materials Science (NIMS) in Japan.

Lead investigator Kenji Yasuda highlighted the broader horizon:
> "By electrically rewriting the structure, we may be able to switch not only magnetism, but also superconductivity and other quantum states, opening a path toward reconfigurable quantum materials and new forms of nonvolatile memory."

Lead author Daniel Brandon underscored the nonvolatility:
> "Once the material is in that state, it persists for a long time and it can go back and forth between the two fairly consistently."

**Scientific Honesty Note**: This study is a laboratory demonstration in an experimental four-layer research device, not a commercial memory chip. Do not mistake it for an off-the-shelf product. The real atomic sliding distance spans only a fraction of a carbon-carbon bond length (~0.14 nm); all interactive animations and layer shifts on this page are schematic illustrations, clearly labeled as such, and are not to-scale atomic trajectories.
