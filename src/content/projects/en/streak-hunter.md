STREAK HUNTER — Exploring the accidental astronomical milestone announced on October 19, 2026 by the European Space Agency (ESA) and the Euclid Consortium: the **Euclid space telescope**, built to construct a 3D cosmic atlas of billions of galaxies and probe dark matter and dark energy, discovered its very first previously unknown comet: **C/2026 S1 (Euclid)**.

A telescope built to photograph the edges of the observable universe inadvertently caught an icy wanderer crossing above our Solar System — simply because over a long exposure, it left an unmistakable streak.

### Euclid's Grand Mission: Mapping Galaxies, Not Hunting Comets

Launched in summer 2023 to the Sun-Earth Lagrange point 2 (L2), Euclid is designed to observe the shapes and motions of billions of galaxies spanning out to 10 billion light-years over six years. By measuring subtle weak-gravitational-lensing distortions and clustering across cosmic epochs, it seeks to reveal the mysterious nature of dark matter and dark energy.

Crucially, **Euclid was not built to track fast-moving Solar System objects**. It is an ultra-stable deep-sky observatory with wide-field visible (VIS) and near-infrared (NISP) instruments tuned to collect faint photons from distant extragalactic realms.

### The Discovery: A Moving Streak Across Deep Field North

Between September 16 and 24, 2026, Euclid surveyed the sky during its ultra-deep Euclid Deep Field North observation campaign. As data flowed through automated processing pipelines, custom-designed moving-object detection software raised a flag: smeared across several images was a distinct linear trail — a "streak."

Following verification, the object was tracked across **5 different fields of view (five image sets)**, with 2 to 4 exposures per field, for a total of **16 exposures** recording its fast motion across the background stars.

### The Discoverer & MPC Official Confirmation

The moving-object detection software was conceived and developed by **Mikko Pöntinen**, a doctoral researcher at the University of Helsinki working within the Euclid Consortium's Solar System Objects Science Working Group.

Upon flagging the anomaly, Pöntinen cross-referenced its coordinates and motion against databases of all known comets and asteroids. None matched. Recognizing that this was a previously undiscovered body, he submitted the astrometric measurements to the International Astronomical Union's **Minor Planet Center (MPC)**. On **October 8, 2026**, the MPC officially confirmed the discovery and assigned its permanent designation: **C/2026 S1 (Euclid)**.

> **Key Distinction**: While Euclid had captured images of known comets previously, **C/2026 S1 (Euclid) is the FIRST previously unknown comet discovered by the observatory**.

### The Smoking Gun: A 560.52-Second VIS Exposure

On September 20, 2026, Euclid's VIS instrument took a single **560.52-second exposure**. Because the comet moved across the detector plane during this extensive duration, its nucleus formed an extended streak.

Surrounding the core trail was an unmistakable diffuse halo: a glowing cloud of dust and outgassing gas — the **coma**. This characteristic fuzz provided conclusive physical proof that this was not a dry, inert asteroid, but an active comet shedding volatile ice under solar irradiation.

### Where It Is: Arching "Straight Up" Over the Planetary Plane

At the time of its discovery, C/2026 S1 (Euclid) was located approximately **1.3 billion kilometres (~1.3 billion km) from Earth** — roughly the distance from the Sun to somewhere between the orbits of Jupiter and Saturn.

Yet it does not travel within the flat ecliptic plane where Earth, Jupiter, and Saturn orbit. From the Sun's viewpoint, the comet is currently positioned almost "straight up", arcing high over the plane of the Solar System on a highly inclined trajectory that will require **thousands of years** to complete one orbit. Its exact orbital period will be refined as future astrometric data is gathered (a precise period should not be fabricated).

### Why Catching It Was Uniquely Difficult

1. **Avoiding the Ecliptic**: To maximize contrast on faint galaxies and minimize zodiacal light (glare from interplanetary dust), Euclid's primary survey specifically steers away from the ecliptic plane where most Solar System objects congregate.
2. **Step-and-Stare Coverage**: Euclid generally visits each sky pointing only once. A moving target cannot simply be revisited on command; catching it in multiple overlapping frames over successive days was a remarkable stroke of observational luck.
3. **Cleaning Galactic Images**: Detecting Solar System objects is not a distraction for Euclid. Identifying and flagging moving streaks allows astronomers to subtract transient light contamination from galactic weak-lensing models, ensuring clean cosmic maps.

### Rehearsal for Comet Interceptor

While this specific comet's steep inclination and immense distance mean it is **not a suitable target** for ESA's upcoming Comet Interceptor mission, the rapid discovery-flag-report pipeline served as a real-world test case for future collaboration. Spotting incoming comets when they are still far from the Sun is precisely what will allow future interceptor spacecraft to prepare, redirect, and encounter pristine dynamic visitors.

### What Comets Tell Us: 4.6-Billion-Year-Old Cryogenic Time Capsules

Comets are icy, dusty leftovers preserved from the birth of the Solar System approximately 4.6 billion years ago, largely stored in the deep chill of the Kuiper Belt and the Oort Cloud. When nudged inward toward the Sun, solar warming vaporizes their volatile ices into an expansive coma and sweeping tail. Because their interiors have remained largely unaltered since the protoplanetary nebula, comets hold vital clues about the building blocks of planets and the origin of Earth's water and organic precursors.
