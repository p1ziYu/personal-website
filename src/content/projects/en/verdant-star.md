Verdant Star · WD J1330+6435 Lab — Exploring humanity's first confirmed genuinely green star: white dwarf **WD J1330+6435**.

Reported in mid-October 2026 (Reuters, University of Warwick; published in *RAS Techniques and Instruments* by lead author Mark Hollands and co-author Boris Gänsicke), astronomers identified an unprecedented stellar relic roughly 280 light-years away in the constellation Draco, with a surface temperature of approximately 4,000 °C. The object is a stellar corpse—the dense leftover core of a low- to intermediate-mass star that exhausted its nuclear fuel and expelled its outer envelopes, packing roughly half the Sun's mass into an Earth-sized volume.

The white dwarf accreted rocky debris from shredded former planetary bodies, enriching its helium-dominated atmosphere with calcium, lithium, and notably large quantities of sodium. In its ultra-dense helium envelope, sodium atoms are incessantly bombarded and perturbed by surrounding helium atoms. This extreme collisional pressure broadens the familiar narrow orange sodium D-line into colossal absorption bands engulfing yellow, orange, and parts of red wavelengths. With those spectral slices subtracted from its intrinsic 4,000 °C thermal continuum, the remaining light shines with a ghostly **chartreuse green glow**. As far as the researchers are aware, no other star, living or dead, has ever been credibly reported as green.

**Scientific Honesty Note**: Nobody has seen this green with human eyes. White dwarfs are intensely faint point sources through even the largest astronomical observatories, showing no resolved discs. The color is reconstructed by combining calibrated stellar spectra with radiative transfer atmospheric models and the standard CIE human color vision observer functions—representing "what it would look like up close."

The same survey also uncovered **WD J1011+2845**, located 48 light-years away in Leo Minor, potentially the reddest star ever recorded: deep convective dredging brought elemental carbon to the surface, where molecular carbon Swan bands extinguish light shorter than 600 nanometres, leaving an intense **salmon-pink glow**. Furthermore, a systematic census of 151 white dwarfs within approximately 65 light-years (20 parsecs) revealed that white remains the overwhelming norm; only 5 displayed unusual chromatic profiles (2 yellow, 3 pink)—roughly 1 in 30 white dwarfs (WD J1330+6435 lies far beyond this local 65-light-year sample at ~280 light-years).

Lead author Mark Hollands (University of Warwick) captured the wonder of this cosmic rarity:
> "Imagine standing on a planet and looking up at a green star. It feels like something out of science fiction."
